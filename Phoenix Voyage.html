The boat. Phoenix do Mar is a 1998 Pacific Seacraft 40 (Crealock design) — 42'2" LOA, 24,000 lb displacement, 8,600 lb ballast, cutter rig, full keel, canoe stern. Rebuilt in Port Townsend, WA before departure. This is a heavy-displacement bluewater cruiser built for seaworthiness and motion comfort over speed, not a planing offshore racer — theoretical hull speed — a limit on speed through the water, not speed over ground — is roughly 7–7.5 kt. Speed through the water (STW) in the 5–6 kt range in moderate breeze represents efficient sailing for this hull. Speed over ground (SOG) can run higher any time current or wave push adds to it, which this log tracks separately via the SOG/STW gap. That distinction matters for every speed/pace judgment in this log.

The voyage. Oliver Widger's stated goal is a ~30,000 nm solo, nonstop, unassisted circumnavigation sail, self-targeted at roughly 8–9 months, by way of the doldrums, the South Atlantic/Pacific westerlies, and a Cape Horn rounding. His longest prior offshore passage was a month-long Oregon–Hawaii solo trip in 2025. He shoved off his home dock, Point Hudson Marina in Port Townsend, on Oct 2 and held overnight in Neah Bay, at the mouth of the Strait of Juan de Fuca, before getting underway outbound at 2:00 PM Oct 3 — this log treats Neah Bay/Oct 3 2:00 PM as Day 0, since that's the open-water start line he appears to be using himself, rather than the dock departure the day before.

Methodology. Positions and boat telemetry come from Oliver's live instrument snapshots, from Entry 13 onward sourced via an automated pull of the tracker's tab=boat view, which carries the same full instrument panel (SOG, STW, true/apparent wind, heading, yaw/pitch/roll, rolling averages, lifetime odometer) as the manual screenshots — the plain position feed is used only as a fallback when that fuller pull isn't available, and is flagged as reduced when it is. The panel's own "Updated" timestamp is unreliable and can lag its actual data by hours; every automated entry is timestamped by matching its displayed position against the position-history array instead, the automated equivalent of manually scrubbing back to the first occurrence of that reading. Weather and sea-state context is drawn only from NWS Offshore/Coastal Waters Forecasts, NDBC buoy observations, and the Ocean Prediction Center's High Seas Forecasts — not from race press or media coverage. OPC's ocean.weather.gov text pages can serve a cached/stale issuance; appending a dummy query string (e.g. ?cachebust=1) reliably forces a fresh pull — use this on every weather check going forward. Rolling averages (24h/7d) are excluded from analysis until enough entries accumulate for them to be meaningful; until then, speed-over-ground is computed directly between consecutive snapshots. Day numbering is anchored to the Oct 3, 2:00 PM PT Neah Bay departure, fixed to that clock rather than shifting with time zones crossed underway: Day 1 begins at departure, and Day N = floor(elapsed hours ÷ 24) + 1. Day 0 is reserved for the pre-departure Neah Bay transit. This replaces an earlier, drifting day-count used in the first several entries, corrected throughout on Oct 19.

Reading the snapshots. The clock at the top of each screenshot is when it was taken (Pacific time); the timestamp above the tracker's time-slider is the timestamp of the data shown, moving in 1-hour increments. A red "NOW" means that data is live to the moment; a grey marker means it's a historical reading for that hour, scrubbed back deliberately. Entries logged here are live reads unless noted otherwise.

Delta window. Every entry's deltas — both the per-instrument changes shown directly on the stat grid (boat speed, course, true wind, wind direction, SOG/STW, heading) and the distance/pace summary below the narrative — are computed against the most recent prior entry that is at least 12 hours older, a sliding window rather than simply "the previous entry." This keeps deltas meaningful even if snapshots are occasionally taken close together (for a specific event of interest); a short gap just slides the comparison point back further until it clears 12 hours. Early in the voyage, before 12+ hours of entries exist, the oldest available entry is used instead.

Entry procedure. For any snapshot taken 8+ hours after the prior entry, the full workup is followed: check live AIS as an independent cross-check, read the snapshot, check current weather/forecast conditions for his position, then write the entry with an added Outlook section covering near-term expectations. Snapshots taken at shorter intervals — typically to capture a specific event, or a deliberate historical back-read rather than a live reading — are logged directly without the full re-check, since not enough time has passed for the broader picture to have moved.

Position descriptor. The plain-language line under each entry's coordinates (e.g. "~83 nm off Point St. George, CA") is a human-readable orientation only — it is computed fresh each entry from the actual lat/long and is never used in any of the quantitative distance/pace figures elsewhere in that entry, which come directly from the coordinates themselves. While he's running roughly parallel to a coastline, it names the single nearest charted point and the perpendicular offshore distance to it. That framing stops applying once he's on an open-ocean leg with no nearby coast (crossing the Pacific toward the equator, the equatorial crossing itself, the Southern Ocean easting after Cape Horn, open South Atlantic/Pacific stretches); for those legs the line switches to a milestone-relative format instead (distance/bearing to the next meaningful waypoint, or a basin/latitude-band description). For any coastal milestone he'll cross the latitude of rather than sail directly toward (a cape, a landmark well inside the coastline), the relevant figure is the along-track distance to close that latitude on his current course — not the straight-line distance to the point itself, which overstates progress by however much of it is westing/easting he isn't actually closing. ("Abeam" was used loosely in earlier entries for this; it's a relative bearing that depends on heading, not what's meant here, which is a fixed latitude or longitude crossing — terminology corrected going forward.) Any ETA to close that gap uses his southing rate (or easting/northing rate, once the relevant axis changes) — the component of velocity actually closing the gap — never raw SOG or STW directly. That's computed either empirically, from the latitude actually closed between two fixes over elapsed time, or instantaneously, by resolving current SOG onto the course direction; both are noted when given. Earlier entries' position lines were written more loosely than this and may be approximate; take the coordinates themselves as the source of truth throughout.
